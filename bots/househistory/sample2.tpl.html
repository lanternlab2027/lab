The LPC’s building database notes that the original owner of No. 70, Adrian Van Sinderen, was the first president of the Brooklyn Savings Bank. [2] Some secondary accounts give a build date of 1834 rather than 1839 [9]. This report follows the city’s 1839 and flags the difference.

IIThe block & neighborhood then

In 1858 a boarding notice placed at No. 70 sold the location as within five minutes’ walk from Fulton or Wall street ferries. [12] The 1887 Sanborn atlas shows the setting. No. 70 is a broad brick dwelling on Willow Street between Orange and Pineapple Streets, and the slope below Columbia Heights runs down to Furman Street. There a wall of bonded warehouses (Harbeck’s Stores, Watson’s Stores) and long piers line the East River, with the Union Ferry Co.’s Fulton Ferry at the north end of the sheet. [11]

  • 1839: Built for Adrian Van Sinderen. [2]
  • 1858 & 1862: Notices offer board, then the lower part of house No. 70 Willow to let. [12][13] (See the numbering caveat below.)
  • 1886: William A. Putnam buys the house, according to Brownstoner’s history of the lot. [9]
  • 1894: Heights society women meet at the house of Mrs. William A. Putnam, 70 Willow street to draw up a protest against woman suffrage. [14]
  • 1919 & 1921: A death and a wedding in the Putnam household are reported from the house. [15][16]
  • 1944: The Putnam children donate the house to the Red Cross. [9]
  • 1953: Stage designer Oliver Smith buys it and owns it until his death in 1994. [9][10]
  • 1955–1965: Truman Capote rents some basement rooms from Smith. [10] His essay on the neighborhood, later known as Brooklyn Heights: A Personal Memoir, first appeared in Holiday in February 1959. [10]
  • 1965: Brooklyn Heights is designated a historic district; the designation is recorded against this lot in ACRIS in Nov 1966. [3][7]

VIn the newspapers

These clippings come from Chronicling America (Library of Congress). Quotations are lightly corrected from OCR, so check each against the page scan. Numbering caveat: We have not confirmed that the 1858 and 1862 notices use today’s house numbers. Those two also sit awkwardly with a single-family Van Sinderen house, so treat them as unverified.

…genteel board on reasonable terms, by applying at No. 70 Willow street, Brooklyn, within five minutes’ walk from Fulton or Wall street ferries.

TO LET—Lower part of house No. 70 Willow street, Brooklyn Heights…
The New York Herald, 27 Jul 1862, p. 6 (to-let notice). [13]
The meeting was held at the house of Mrs. William A. Putnam, 70 Willow street, and two dozen society women were present.
The Sun, 22 Apr 1894, p. 4 (on an anti-suffrage protest organized on the Heights). [14]
