What the exam covers

  • The exam focuses on general New York City knowledge, predominantly Manhattan, but includes questions about all five boroughs. (DCWP Sightseeing Guide Study Reference p. 1)
  • History runs from the Algonquin Indians to the present day, and the Study Reference asks for an understanding of the 1811 “grid plan” and its impacts. (DCWP Sightseeing Guide Study Reference p. 1)
  • The literature topic is limited to nine authors: E. B. White, Langston Hughes, Edith Wharton, Washington Irving, Paule Marshall, Jack Finney, Walt Whitman, O. Henry and F. Scott Fitzgerald. (DCWP Sightseeing Guide Study Reference p. 1)
  • Tour bus routings and pick-up/drop-off rules and the Midtown Thru Streets Program are marked “very important,” with special attention to NYC DOT bus regulations. (DCWP Sightseeing Guide Study Reference p. 2)

The license and the exam

  • Applicants must be at least 18. The exam has 150 questions and you need at least 97 correct. The $50 exam fee is nonrefundable and separate from the license fee. (DCWP Sightseeing Guide License Application Checklist)
  • ExamBuilder runs the exam. You have 60 days to take and pass it once your information appears in ExamBuilder's system. (DCWP Licensing FAQs: Exams p. 1; DCWP Sightseeing Guide License Application Checklist)
  • After a failed attempt, wait 24 hours before booking the second. To retake after that, pay the $50 fee again. A missed exam without rescheduling by 5:00 p.m. the day before is an automatic fail. (DCWP Licensing FAQs: Exams p. 3; DCWP Licensing FAQs: Exams p. 2)
  • The license runs 2 years and expires March 31 in even years. Only new applicants take the exam. (DCWP Sightseeing Guide License Application Checklist; DCWP Licensing FAQs: Exams p. 1)

Tour bus rules (NYC DOT)

  • Buses carry a route slip with the origin, destination and streets in between, and travel on NYC truck routes except to reach a destination. Parkways and the FDR Drive need a Highway Travel Permit. (NYC DOT: Charter & Tour Buses)
  • Bus meters cost $20 an hour with a three-hour limit, in effect 7 am to 7 pm daily except Sundays. Buses cannot park in Lower Manhattan for more than three hours. (NYC DOT: Charter & Tour Buses)
  • No pick-ups or drop-offs in a travel lane or a “No Stopping Anytime” zone; operators stay with the bus while passengers load; double-parking is never allowed. At passenger drop-off zones, idling is strictly prohibited above 40°F. (NYC DOT: Charter & Tour Buses)
  • In DOT's holiday-season motor coach rules, buses may use all river crossings except the Brooklyn Bridge, and may not pick up or drop off on Fifth Avenue between 32nd and 59th Streets. (NYC DOT: Charter & Tour Buses)

Bridges and the ferry

  • The Brooklyn Bridge, designed by John A. Roebling, was built 1869–1883 and was then the longest suspension bridge in the world. It is the oldest bridge open to traffic. (NYC DOT: Brooklyn Bridge; NYC DOT: Bridges)
  • The Williamsburg Bridge (1903) was the longest suspension bridge in the world when it opened. The Manhattan Bridge (1909), designed by Carrère and Hastings, is the youngest of the three DOT East River suspension bridges. (NYC DOT: Williamsburg Bridge; NYC DOT: Manhattan Bridge)
  • The Ed Koch Queensboro (59th Street) Bridge, by Gustav Lindenthal, is a cantilever truss bridge, the only one of the four great East River bridges that is not a suspension bridge. (NYC DOT: Ed Koch Queensboro Bridge)
  • NYC's first bridge, the King's Bridge, was built in 1693. NYC DOT bridges have no tolls. (NYC DOT: Bridges)
  • The City has run the free Staten Island Ferry since 1905: a 5.2-mile run from St. George to Whitehall, 24 hours a day, 365 days a year. (NYC DOT: Staten Island Ferry Facts; NYC DOT: Staten Island Ferry)

History and Gracie Mansion

  • In 1624, 30 Dutch families began to settle Manhattan. The Dutch West India Company ran the colony until 1664, when English soldiers took the town and New Amsterdam became New York. (A Visit to Gracie Mansion: Resource Guide for Teachers and Docents p. 13)
  • 1827: the city's first public transit, a 12-seat horse-drawn bus. 1904: the IRT runs its first subway train. (A Visit to Gracie Mansion: Resource Guide for Teachers and Docents p. 15)
  • Archibald Gracie built his house in 1799. It became the official mayor's residence in 1942, and Fiorello H. La Guardia was the first mayor to live there. (Gracie Mansion: A Student Guide to the People's House p. 3; Gracie Mansion: A Student Guide to the People's House p. 9)

Landmarks and civic buildings

  • City Hall has been the seat of city government since 1812; its architects were Joseph François Mangin and John McComb, Jr. (DCAS: City Hall)
  • Tweed Courthouse is the legacy of Tammany boss William M. Tweed, who was convicted there in 1873. Today it is the Department of Education headquarters. (DCAS: Tweed Courthouse)
  • The Landmarks Preservation Commission was created in 1965 under Mayor Robert F. Wagner, after losses such as Pennsylvania Station (McKim, Mead & White, 1911; demolished 1963). It has 11 commissioners appointed by the Mayor. (Landmarks Preservation Commission: About LPC)

Culture and immigration

  • The city's Cultural Institutions Group partnership began in 1869 with the American Museum of Natural History. The Percent for Art Law dedicates one percent of eligible City-funded construction budgets to public art. (DCLA: Cultural Institutions Group; NYC Department of Cultural Affairs: History)
  • In City Planning's 2013 Newest New Yorkers, the Dominican Republic was the largest source of the city's foreign-born in 2011, followed by China. (NYC Planning: The Newest New Yorkers (2013), Chapter 1 p. 2)
